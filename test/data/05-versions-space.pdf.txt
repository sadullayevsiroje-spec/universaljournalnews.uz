

FACTORS AFFECTING THE DEVELOPMENT OF INTESTINAL MICROFLORA IN 
CHILDREN 
Ibraximova H.R., Atadjanova O.N., Artikova D.O., Faxriddinova D.M. 
 
Urgench State Medical Institute, Urgench, Uzbekistan. 
 
Abstract: This article analyzes the formation of the gut microbiome in children, its impact 
on  the  body,  and  the  main  influencing  factors.  The  role  of  birth  mode,  feeding  type,  and 
environmental conditions in the development of intestinal microbiota is also discussed. 
Keywords: gut  microbiota,  immune  system,  bifidobacteria,  lactobacilli,  child  health, 
nutrition. 
The  development  of  the  intestinal  microbiota  in  children  is  a  complex  biological  process 
influenced  by  multiple  stages  and  shaped  by  genetic,  physiological,  and  environmental  factors. 
This process begins during the prenatal period and continues under the influence of factors such as 
the  mode  of  delivery,  nutritional  regimen,  antibiotic  therapy,  environmental  conditions,  and 
hygiene  and  sanitation  standards.  The  early  formation  of  the  child’s  microbiome  lays  the 
foundation  for  the  development  of  the  immune  system,  metabolic  processes,  internal  organ 
function,  and  the  body’s  ability  to  resist  various  diseases. According  to  current  data,  natural 
(vaginal)  birth  creates  optimal  conditions  for  the  healthy  and  balanced development of a child’s 
gut microbiota. In this case, the child acquires the mother’s vaginal and intestinal flora, which 
serve  as  a  foundation  for  the  formation  of  their  own  microbiome.  In  contrast,  children  born  via 
cesarean  section  are  colonized  primarily  by  microbes  from  the  surrounding  environment  and 
hospital  setting,  leading  to  deviations  from  normal  microbiota  composition  and,  in  some  cases, 
associated   health   complications. Feeding   type   is   also   a   key   determinant   in   microbiome 
development.   Breastfeeding   supports   the   enrichment   of   the   microbiome   with   beneficial 
bifidobacteria   and  lactobacilli  while  reducing   the  number  of  pathogenic  and  conditionally 
pathogenic bacteria. Breast milk contains immunologically active components such as lactoferrin, 
oligosaccharides,  secretory  IgA,  probiotic  bacteria,  and  others,  which  play  a  significant  role  in 
modulating the infant’s immune system and maintaining microbiome stability. Moreover,  early 
and  inappropriate  use  of  antibiotics  has  been  shown  to  negatively  affect  the  development  of  the 
microbiome, reducing its resistance to various diseases. This impact is particularly pronounced in 
the early weeks and months after birth, during which the microbiota is still in the formative stage. 
Recent  studies  confirm  that  microbial  imbalance  (dysbiosis)  in  children  can  affect  not  only  the 
functioning of internal organs but also brain development, psychological state, and neuroendocrine 
system  function.  This  positions  microbiome  research  as  one  of  the  most  promising  directions in 
pediatrics and general medicine. 
Conclusion: The  formation  of  the  intestinal  microbiota  in  children  is  a  multifactorial, 
complex, and long-term process. Each stage directly affects the child’s immunological, metabolic, 
and  physiological  development.  Scientific  evidence  suggests  that  the  initial  formation  of  the 
microbiota  begins  not  only  during  delivery  but  also  in  the  prenatal  period.  Therefore,  maternal 
health—especially the state of maternal microbiocenosis during pregnancy—plays a decisive role 
in determining the child’s future microbiome composition. The balanced and proper development 

of intestinal microflora plays a critical role in stimulating the immune system, providing effective 
defense   against   pathogenic   flora,   absorbing   nutrients,   synthesizing   vitamins,   and   ensuring 
metabolic  functions.  Disruptions  in  microbiota  development  can  increase  the  risk  of  allergies, 
autoimmune  diseases,  obesity,  type  1  and  type  2  diabetes,  functional  bowel  disorders,  and  other 
complex  conditions  in  children. Key  factors  influencing  microbiome  development  include  the 
mode  of  delivery,  type  of  feeding  (especially  breastfeeding  or  formula  feeding),  sanitary  and 
hygiene  conditions,  use  of  antibiotics,  and  geographical  environment.  Vaginal  delivery  and 
breastfeeding  create  optimal  biological  conditions  for  the  child's  health.  Hence,  it  is  essential  to 
develop  preventive  and  clinical  guidelines  that  support  the  proper  formation  of  the  child’s 
microbiota  based  on  these  factors. Future  research  should  focus  on  a  deeper  understanding  of 
pediatric  microbiome  development  and  the  creation  of  individualized  approaches  for  each  child. 
Developing nutrition- and therapy-based models grounded in microbiome science could mark the 
beginning  of  a  new  era  in  medicine.  In  this  context,  monitoring  the  microbiome,  scientifically 
validated  and  targeted  use  of  prebiotics  and  probiotics,  and  the  rational  use  of  antibiotics  are 
strategic  priorities. In  conclusion,  the  healthy  and  balanced  development  of  gut  microbiota  in 
children  is  a  fundamental factor  not  only  for  their  childhood  health  but  also  for  long-term 
physiological   stability   and   disease   resistance   throughout   life.   Comprehensive   analysis   and 
regulation of the influencing factors can significantly enhance the effectiveness of prevention and 
treatment strategies in pediatric practice.
